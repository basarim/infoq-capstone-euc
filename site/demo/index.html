Is it still doing what the business intended?

Six months after launch, the team has revised the prompts, replaced the model, and changed how the application retrieves information. Each change passed the existing tests. Yet the team still cannot tell whether the system serves the original business goal. An Executable Use Case addresses this gap by recording what the business needs in a machine-readable form, giving the implementation and its evaluation the same reference point.

Business Intent → EUC → Implementation + Evaluation

One reference, not two interpretations of it.

01The problem

How do we know the system still serves the original business goal?

To understand the uncertainty, follow the business goal from the original requirement to the behaviour running in production. Three parts of that chain need to remain connected.

Part of the chain

Business requirement

States the goal and the rules the business expects the application to follow.

Part of the chain

Implementation

Turns the requirement into code, prompts, context, and model behaviour. It changes as the application evolves.

Part of the chain

Evaluation

Checks whether the resulting behaviour meets defined criteria. It may continue to pass when those criteria no longer represent the original requirement.

The problem is not that any one part is missing. It is that teams maintain each part separately, making the links between them easy to lose. The implementation and evaluation can remain consistent with each other while both move away from the business goal.

In a traditional software development lifecycle, teams translate requirements into code and test whether known inputs produce expected outputs. When behaviour changes, the code or its configuration usually provides a traceable cause.

An AI development lifecycle, however, introduces more sources of change. The prompt, model, and retrieved context all influence the application's behaviour, while the evaluation criteria determine how that behaviour is judged. A change to any one of them may alter the result without causing a conventional software failure. Tests can therefore continue to pass even after what they measure has drifted from the original business requirement.

A passing evaluation shows that the application met the test. It does not show that the test still represents the business goal.

If the connection between the business requirement, implementation, and evaluation is lost, the problem may become visible only after the application reaches production. Resolving it means tracing the observed behaviour through the evaluation and implementation to the original requirement. That work becomes harder when decisions are scattered across artifacts or the people who made them are no longer available. Teams therefore need a shared reference that preserves those decisions and keeps all three parts connected as the application evolves.

02The idea

Jacobson's use case, extended for AI-native engineering.

Introduced by Ivar Jacobson in the 1980s and formalised in 1992, a use case describes how an actor works with a system to achieve a goal while separating required behaviour from implementation. That separation remains useful in AI-native engineering, where prompts, models, and retrieval methods can change while the business goal remains stable.

AI-native engineering adds a new traceability need to that foundation. As prompts, models, and retrieval methods change, teams need to keep both the implementation and its evaluation connected to the business goal. This project addresses that need by extending, rather than replacing, Jacobson's actor-and-goal framing.

An Executable Use Case makes that extension concrete. It retains the actor and goal, expresses the required business behaviour in a machine-readable form, and links each evaluation criterion to the requirement, rule, or policy it validates. It does not prescribe a prompt, model, retrieval strategy, framework, or code structure. Those implementation choices can change while both the application and its evaluation remain connected to the same business reference.

Industry challenge — Forward Deployed Engineering and AI consulting

Forward Deployed Engineers and AI consultants turn a client's business need into a production system. As prompts, models, context, and integrations change, the delivery team's technical definition of success may drift from the client's original expectation. Both sides may believe the project is on track while working toward different definitions of "done."

An EUC gives both sides a shared meaning for delivery: one machine-readable definition of the goal, requirements, acceptable outcomes, and evaluation criteria. The client reviews what success means in business terms, while the delivery team binds implementation and evaluation to the same named requirements. When the system changes, both can see whether the business intent changed or only the way the system meets it. This makes alignment explicit, exposes disagreements earlier, and may reduce rework caused by different definitions of "done."

03The artifact

What is an Executable Use Case (EUC)?

An EUC brings the business goal, the behaviour the application must provide, and the criteria used to judge it into one machine-readable artifact. Here, executable does not mean that the EUC runs the application. It means software can validate the artifact and use its contents to guide implementation, evaluation, and release decisions. The committed Grant Fit Assessment shows those parts as real objects contained within the same EUC. The structure defines what belongs to the artifact; the links between the objects make it traceable.

The key link is tracesTo. Each evaluation criterion names the requirement, rule, or policy it validates, so an evaluation result can be followed back to a specific piece of business intent.

An EUC applies first-principles thinking: start with business intent, then derive the required behaviour, constraints, and evaluation criteria from it. Those criteria can anchor downstream evaluation artifacts — curated datasets, often called Golden Sets, scoring rubrics, and regression tests — so each remains connected to the intent it is meant to protect.

Two things are worth reading off the structure.

Policies apply to the use case as a whole, not to a single requirement. For example, "Do not invent missing information" constrains every step that relies on model judgement, not only the alignment assessment. This structure draws on Jacobson and Ng's work on cross-cutting concerns — rules whose scope spans multiple steps.

Evaluation stays connected to business requirements, not implementation choices. In the committed EUC, EVAL-ALIGNMENT traces to ALIGNMENT-001 and RULE-ELIGIBILITY-PRECEDES-FIT; it does not name a prompt, model, or test file. The team can therefore change how alignment is implemented while continuing to judge the result against the same business requirement and rule.

Actor

04Case study

A real grant decision requires both fixed rules and model judgement.

An EUC becomes concrete when it guides a real business decision. Before a nonprofit commits staff time to an application, it needs to decide whether the grant is worth pursuing. The prototype captures that decision in the Grant Fit Assessment, an Executable Use Case that records the business intent in a machine-readable form. The application then uses its requirements to guide runtime behaviour.

The assessment asks two questions in sequence: does the organization meet the grant's explicit eligibility rules, and, if so, how well do its mission and programs align with the funder's priorities? Fixed rules settle the first question; model judgement handles the second.

Is the organization eligible?

Explicit grant requirements determine a pass or fail. Changing a rule represents a deliberate change to the business requirement.

  • Mandatory eligibility
  • Geographic scope
  • Required information
  • Eligibility before fit

How well does the grant align?

A model weighs the available evidence, classifies the fit, and explains its judgement.

  • Mission alignment
  • Program alignment
  • Supporting evidence
  • Uncertainty

Both answers inform the same decision, but eligibility comes first. The EUC makes that order explicit through RULE-ELIGIBILITY-PRECEDES-FIT, which states that strong alignment cannot overcome a failed mandatory requirement. EVAL-ALIGNMENT traces back to the same rule, keeping the evaluation criterion connected to the business constraint that governs runtime behaviour.

The prototype puts this rule into practice. If a mandatory requirement fails, the assessment returns POOR_FIT before the model is invoked. This avoids an unnecessary model call. More importantly, it prevents model judgement from overriding an explicit eligibility rule. The interaction diagram below shows how the runtime applies the rule and how the evaluator reads its criteria from the same EUC.

The diagram follows one grant assessment from request to evaluation. The routing agent reads the EUC once, then uses each requirement's type to choose the appropriate path. Fixed requirements go to the rules evaluator; the reasoned requirement invokes the model. If an eligibility rule fails, the assessment returns POOR_FIT before the model is invoked. Once execution is complete, the evaluator retrieves the relevant criteria from the same EUC, keeping each verdict traceable to the business requirement it checks.

05The boundary

The EUC defines the work; it does not perform it.

The Grant Fit case study shows an EUC guiding runtime behaviour, but the EUC is not the runtime. It defines what the application must do and how its results should be judged. The implementation and evaluation tools still perform the work: the EUC retrieves no information, controls no model reasoning, and runs no evaluations.

That separation is deliberate. If the EUC retrieved information, invoked a model, or ran evaluations, a change to any of those mechanisms could also force the business artifact to change. Keeping them outside allows the EUC to remain stable while the implementation evolves, unless the business intent itself changes. Teams can continue using tools such as DeepEval or Ragas, mapping each EUC criterion to a deterministic assertion, a standard metric, or a rubric scored by a model.

The same boundary applies to the artifacts those tools use. A Golden Set remains outside the EUC, but it is derived from it: each scenario and expected outcome should exercise a named requirement or evaluation criterion. Teams likewise build scoring rubrics and regression tests from, or map them to, the EUC's criteria, then run them with their chosen evaluation framework. These tools and artifacts can evolve while the EUC preserves the business definition against which their results are interpreted.

The diagram places the EUC between business intent and the systems that implement and evaluate it. When that intent remains unchanged, the EUC serves as the shared reference: its requirements guide implementation, and its criteria guide evaluation. The components in the dashed boxes can change independently while each evaluation result remains traceable to the requirement it assesses.

Evaluation measures what the system did. The EUC connects that result to what the business needs.
06The experiment

Can the EUC tell a safe change from a business regression?

The demo uses the Grant Fit Assessment, a use case that helps a nonprofit decide whether a grant is worth the staff time required to pursue it. The assessment first checks whether the organization meets the grant's mandatory eligibility rules. Only eligible organizations proceed to a model-based assessment of how well their mission and programs align with the funder's priorities.

Against that baseline, the demo simulates routine changes to the AI implementation. The prompt may be revised, the model replaced, or the context updated, while the business expectations recorded in the EUC remain fixed. If the organization fails a mandatory eligibility rule, the assessment must not recommend pursuing the grant. For an eligible organization, the fit decision must reflect the funder's priorities, use available evidence, and surface uncertainty. The demo asks whether evaluation linked to those expectations can tell a safe implementation change from a business regression.

To keep the experiment focused, it uses one EUC and a small Golden Set derived from its requirements and evaluation criteria — the same six cases shown on The EUC tab. Both remain fixed while each run changes one part of the implementation. The baseline establishes the expected behaviour, and the same mapped evaluators assess every variant. A variant that preserves the business requirements should pass; when it violates them, the evaluation should identify the failed criterion and trace it back to the originating requirement, rule, or policy.

The demo measures both missed regressions and false alarms. If evaluation passes a business violation, it is not protecting the requirement it was designed to check. If it repeatedly flags safe changes, the criteria may be too closely tied to one implementation. Either result shows what must improve before EUC-linked evaluation can support a reliable release decision.

07Why EUC matters for AI Engineering

A shared definition of success, from discovery to release.

An EUC provides a stable reference throughout the lifecycle of an AI capability. Before development begins, it makes the required outcomes, evidence, rules, and evaluation criteria explicit enough for business stakeholders, clients, and technical teams to review together. That shared definition of "done" can expose mismatched assumptions before they become implementation rework, strengthen client confidence, and provide a clearer basis for acceptance.

Once agreed, the same EUC carries those decisions into delivery. Its machine-readable requirements can guide implementation, including AI-assisted development, while its evaluation criteria can be used to derive a Golden Set and regression tests. As prompts, models, and context change, the derived tests and mapped evaluators can check whether the capability still meets the agreed intent and trace a failed criterion to the originating requirement. This does not make the system predictable; it makes the effects of change easier to evaluate and investigate. That continuity matters because much of the effort involved in AI delivery lies beyond model development, as the figures below show.

~15%

of a typical GenAI project's effort may be the model itself, McKinsey reports. Most delivery work sits elsewhere.

$3:$1

change-management spend for every dollar of model development, according to McKinsey's experience.

≥50%

of GenAI projects had been abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, Gartner reported, citing unclear value, inadequate controls, poor data, or rising costs.

30–50%

faster use-case development is associated with reusable code, per McKinsey. This capstone asks whether business intent and evaluation criteria can be reused too.

These figures do not measure the impact of an EUC directly. They show why reuse, alignment, and control beyond the model matter. An EUC is intended to address a narrow part of that work: repeated requirements clarification, late disagreements about acceptance, evaluation redesign, and regression investigation when a shared definition of success is lost.

That shared definition can extend to release and operation. If the experiment supports the approach, evaluation linked to the EUC could contribute to a release gate alongside existing technical and operational checks. A failed criterion would identify the business requirement at risk, while a pass would provide traceable evidence that the change still meets the agreed intent. With appropriate production monitoring and observability, the same links could connect post-deployment drift or failure to the originating requirement, making troubleshooting easier.

An EUC does not promise a risk-free release. It gives each release a stable business standard and traceable evidence against it.

This capstone examines whether the EUC can function as a first-class artifact of the AI SDLC — a durable, machine-readable expression of business intent that remains connected to how an AI capability is designed, built, evaluated, released, and monitored. Rather than allowing intent to fragment across documents, prompts, code, and test cases, the EUC provides a common reference that can persist as the system evolves. The prototype is intended to demonstrate that continuity: business intent can remain explicit, traceable, and actionable throughout the lifecycle.

That same structure may improve AI-assisted development and token economy. By recording the goal, constraints, evidence, and acceptance criteria once, a developer can ask an AI coding tool to implement a registered EUC instead of rebuilding the requirement through repeated prompts. A complete initial brief may reduce back-and-forth and token use, although success should be measured by the delivered outcome and total tokens rather than assumed from a one-shot attempt. That precision does not imply waterfall delivery. It supports agile development and thin slicing: stakeholders can refine the EUC registry as they learn, then implement and validate one small, valuable use case at a time.

→Read on

Models will improve, prompts will evolve, retrieval will be replaced. The business intent should not have to be rediscovered every time.

The contribution is not the grant application. It is a domain-independent pattern and a falsifiable claim about it: that a fixed, machine-readable statement of intent can keep implementation and evaluation connected while everything underneath them changes.

The Benefits

      
None of this is worth the extra structure unless it changes something for the people who have to live with the system after it ships. Here is what a machine-readable use case is meant to buy.


      

        

          Shared definition of done
          
One artifact, spanning the AI SDLC

          
An EUC is a first-class, structured, machine-readable artifact that defines the business goal, governing rules and policies, execution steps, and success criteria — giving business and technical stakeholders a shared way to negotiate and preserve what "done" means.

        

        

          Thin-slice delivery
          
One bounded capability at a time

          
Each EUC represents a bounded business capability, supporting agile thin-slice delivery: one meaningful end-to-end use case can be designed, implemented, tested, and validated at a time.

        

        

          Coding-agent effectiveness
          
Less prompting, more doing

          
Its structured format improves coding-agent effectiveness and token economy. Instead of restating requirements in a long prompt, a developer can say "Implement EUC: The Fit Assessment" — the agent works from the EUC's own context, constraints, behavior, and acceptance criteria, reducing repeated prompting and back-and-forth.

        

        

          Bounding probabilistic behavior
          
Less surface area for uncertainty

          
Generative AI is inherently probabilistic. EUCs help reduce the surface area exposed to that behavior by making business intent explicit, constraining expected behavior, and defining how results are verified.

The static shape

      
The EUC defines the execution requirements and business criteria, while the graph engine provides the orchestration needed to carry them out. Each requirement is associated with an implementation component, or filter, responsible for performing the work and interacting with the shared execution context.

      
This separation keeps the EUC focused on what the business requires, the graph engine focused on coordinating the process, and filters focused on how each requirement is implemented. Because these responsibilities are separated, implementations can evolve without changing the EUC or its business intent.

    After deployment
    
      
    Latency, errors, and uptime can all stay green while the system quietly stops doing what the business asked for.
    
      
    After deployment, the EUC continues to serve as the definition of expected behavior. Production traces and outcomes can be evaluated against the same rules, criteria, and acceptance conditions established before development, helping teams detect when system behavior no longer aligns with business intent. Rather than monitoring only technical signals such as latency, errors, or availability, EUC-driven monitoring asks a more important question: is the system still doing what the business intended?
    
    
    

    
    
      
    The same EUC, now observed
    
      
    Checking a live request against the same rules and criteria means running the same code, not standing up a parallel monitoring system. Langfuse wraps the exact reasoner, deterministic gates, and evaluator this demo already uses — nothing about GrantFitApplication, the pipeline, or either evaluator changes. Each deterministic gate becomes a traced check; the reasoned step becomes a traced model call, complete with the prompt, the response, and the real token count Claude reports back; the same three criteria from the Evaluation tab above are attached to that exact run as scores.
    
      
    If a request halts at a gate — say, an applicant that isn't a registered nonprofit — the trace shows exactly that: the eligibility check ran and failed, and nothing downstream of it ran at all. No case ever produces an empty trace. Every trace also carries the EUC's own id, so every trace this EUC has ever produced can be found together, and a fresh correlation id generated for that one request, so a specific run can be found again from outside Langfuse entirely — a log line, a support ticket, another system's own trace.
